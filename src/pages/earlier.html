Finding a Roman pigment with night-vision goggles

MIT course on Roman Pompeii, on site in January 2026

Egyptian Blue, a pigment the Romans used, absorbs visible light and glows in the near infrared, around 900 to 1,000 nm. Modified night-vision goggles can see that glow, so traces too faint to see by eye show up on the walls. On a three-person team in MIT's course on Roman Pompeii, we set out to make that survey faster and cleaner.

{{fig:earlier/goggles.png|Two hands holding a night-vision unit with its screen facing the camera|The modified night-vision unit.|}}

The existing method subtracts one unlit photo from one lit photo. Lighting from one side leaves half the image brighter than the other, and stray infrared from the lamp lights up the wall's texture as noise.

My part was the code for our strobe method. It takes a few seconds of 4K video from the goggles while a light strobes, stacks the frames into one array and sorts every pixel's brightness over time. It throws away the darkest 15%, the brightest 15% and the middle 40%, which are the frames caught mid-flash, then averages what is left into one synthetic lit frame and one unlit frame and subtracts them.

One short video replaces a set of carefully aligned photos, which is what makes surveying a whole site plausible. The cost is that fine detail can be lost.

Team: Coleman Duplessie, Angelo Chaca, Joshua Curhan. Python, NumPy, OpenCV.

The orbit of a near-Earth asteroid from twelve telescope images

Summer Science Program, 2024

Three of us measured the orbit of asteroid (352143) 2007 LR32 from our own observations: twelve usable images over four nights on 0.4 m telescopes at Las Cumbres Observatory.

The pipeline, in Python, finds the asteroid's centroid in each image, fits plate constants against ten reference stars by least squares to get its sky position, and runs the Method of Gauss on three observations to get the six orbital elements. A jackknife over 108 triples of observations picked the set to use. Each of us then ran the orbit determination and a 10,000-sample Monte Carlo for the uncertainties independently, with our own code.

ElementMy resultDifference from JPL
Semi-major axis2.593 ± 0.085 au1.2%
Eccentricity0.511 ± 0.0131.0%
Inclination10.12 ± 0.19°0.6%

On those three elements my code landed closer to JPL's values than either teammate's, with the widest error bars. Our three scripts, fed the same data, often disagreed by more than their stated uncertainties, which the report says plainly. We submitted the observations to the Minor Planet Center.

MIT Rocket Team, fluid systems

August 2025 to May 2026

Project Osiris is a nitrogen-pressurized, throttleable liquid engine with nitrous oxide as its oxidizer and servo-driven ball valves. I designed servo-valve mounts for it in SolidWorks, and drafted failure-state procedures for the throttle-valve characterization test: what to do at each step if a valve fails to open or fails to close, and a general abort.

Nuclear forensics research

MIT Nuclear Science and Engineering, December 2025 to September 2026

An NNSA-funded project that uses flash calorimetry to read the energy stored in irradiated ceramics as a record of their dose history. I debugged the lab's MATLAB enthalpy pipeline, tracing consistently low results to a baseline window that overlapped the start of melting, and validated a Monte Carlo method for estimating sample mass on nanogram indium samples.
